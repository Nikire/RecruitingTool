Bullhorn is the default answer when a staffing agency asks what software to buy. It has been in the market for two decades, it runs some very large staffing firms, and its name comes up in every procurement conversation.

It is also the reason a lot of five-person agencies are still running on spreadsheets.

## Why Bullhorn is hard to evaluate at small scale

Bullhorn does not publish list pricing. To get a number you go through a sales process: a discovery call, a demo, a scoping conversation about seats and modules, and then a quote. That is a completely normal enterprise motion, and if you are a 200-recruiter firm with a dedicated ops lead it is the right one — you genuinely do need scoping.

If you are five people, three of whom also carry a desk, it means:

- You cannot compare it to anything without spending several hours in calls.
- The quote is typically **per user, annual, with a term commitment**, so the number you are shown is a yearly obligation rather than a monthly experiment.
- Implementation and data migration are usually their own line, and their own project.
- The modules that make Bullhorn _Bullhorn_ — the wider staffing suite around the core ATS/CRM — are what push the total up.

None of that is a criticism of the product. It is a statement about fit. Enterprise staffing software is priced and sold for enterprise staffing firms, and a small agency evaluating it is trying to buy a category of product that was not designed for its size.

## What a small staffing agency actually needs

Before you shop, be honest about the shortlist. Across the small agencies we talk to, the real requirements are consistently these:

1. **One place where every candidate and every submission lives.** Not four spreadsheets and a shared inbox.
2. **A careers page that produces inbound applications**, so you are not sourcing 100% of every role.
3. **Resume screening that survives a 90-application week** without someone reading all ninety.
4. **Interview scheduling that does not consume a coordinator.**
5. **Candidate email that goes out consistently** — including rejections, because your reputation in a small market is your pipeline.
6. **Numbers you can show a client**: time to hire, funnel conversion, where the good candidates came from.
7. **A price you can cancel.**

Notice what is not on that list: VMS integration, back-office pay-and-bill, timesheet capture, a full CRM with commission reporting. If those _are_ on your list, be aware that most sub-$100/month tools will not have them — and that is exactly the decision point between this article's recommendations and staying with an enterprise suite.

## How to evaluate alternatives without wasting a month

**Insist on published pricing.** If a vendor will not show you a number on a web page, you are entering a sales process, not a purchase. That is a legitimate choice, but make it deliberately.

**Check the seat model before the feature list.** Per-seat pricing is the mechanism that turns a $29 tool into a $400 monthly bill by next year. A flat per-firm price behaves differently as you grow. Do the arithmetic at the headcount you expect in twelve months, not today's.

**Test the careers page markup yourself.** Publish a test role, open the public page, and run it through Google's Rich Results Test. If the vendor's job pages do not emit valid `JobPosting` structured data, they are not eligible for the Google Jobs experience, and one of the main reasons to have a hosted careers page has quietly disappeared. See our [JSON-LD guide](/blog/google-jobs-schema-json-ld-guide) for what a correct implementation contains.

**Send yourself the emails.** Trigger a real application, a real rejection, and a real interview invitation during the trial. Candidate-facing email is where cheap tools are cheapest, and it is the surface your market judges you on.

**Ask what happens to your data on the way out.** Export to CSV, resume file download, API access. Ask before you sign, not when you leave.

## What Borderless does not do

This matters more than the list above, because the wrong purchase is expensive in both directions.

- **No back-office.** No pay-and-bill, no timesheets, no invoicing, no commission tracking. If you place contractors and need to bill hours, you need something else, or something in addition.
- **No VMS or job-board distribution network.** You publish to your own careers board. Multi-posting to external boards is not built in.
- **No sourcing browser extension.** There is no one-click capture from LinkedIn.
- **No CRM for client-side business development** in the sense a staffing suite means it — pipelines are candidate-side.
- **It is a young product.** Fewer integrations, shorter track record, smaller community than a twenty-year incumbent.

If three or more of those are dealbreakers, an enterprise staffing suite is genuinely the correct purchase and you should go take the sales call.

## Other alternatives worth shortlisting

To be useful rather than self-serving, the small-agency market has several credible options and you should look at more than one:

- **Zoho Recruit** — publishes per-recruiter pricing, has a free tier for a single recruiter, and benefits from the wider Zoho ecosystem if you already use Zoho. Per-recruiter pricing means the same seat math applies as you grow.
- **Recruit CRM, Loxo, JobAdder** and similar mid-market tools — closer to Bullhorn in staffing-specific workflow, generally priced per user.
- **Manatal** — strong on sourcing and enrichment; see our [Manatal comparison](/blog/manatal-alternative-borderless-vs-manatal).

Shortlist three, trial two, and buy on workflow rather than on the feature grid. Every vendor's grid has every checkbox.

## A sensible evaluation plan

Give yourself two weeks, not two months.

**Week one:** pick one live requisition. Set it up end to end in the tool — job position, stages, careers page publish, three real candidates. Do not import history yet.

**Week two:** run real candidate traffic through it. Send the emails. Schedule an interview through the candidate-facing booking flow. Pull the funnel report at the end and see whether the numbers are ones you would show a client.

If the tool survived a real requisition, it will survive your business. If it did not, you have lost two weeks rather than an annual contract.
